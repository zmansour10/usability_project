Foundations of Visual Perception
Preattentive processing
- Occurs before focused attention
- Automatically scans the entire visual field
- Fast, effortless & parallel
- Improves intuitiveness and natural information intake

--- PAGE 4 ---
Examples of Preattentive processing
[Image Description: Two identical dense blocks of random numbers. In the first block, all numbers are the same normal font weight and color, making it very hard to count the 3s. In the second block, the number '3' is bolded, making it instantly pop out from the rest of the numbers, demonstrating how preattentive processing spots differences in font weight instantly.]

--- PAGE 5 ---
Semantic depth of field (SDOF)
[Image Description: Two fields of scattered dots. On the left, all dots are slightly blurry. On the right, one single dot in the lower middle area is sharp and dark, while the others are blurry. This immediately draws the eye to the sharp dot.]
- Uses blur and sharpness as preattentive features
- Directs users' attention to relevant objects

--- PAGE 6 ---
Preattentive Attributes
- Perceived in under 10 ms
- Used by our working memory

Table of Attributes:
- Form: line orientation, line length, width, size, curvature, spatial grouping, added marks, spatial position.
- Color: hue, intensity.
- Motion: flicker, motion direction.
- Spatial position: 2D position, stereoscopic depth, convexity/concavity.

--- PAGE 10 & 11 ---
The Gestalt Principles of grouping
Gestalt principles explain how the eye creates a whole (gestalt) out of individual elements.
[Image Description: 
- Proximity: Grids of dots arranged so they look like vertical columns instead of rows because they are closer vertically.
- Similarity: Grids of hollow and filled dots looking like horizontal rows because similar colors group together.
- Common region: Dots enclosed in a drawn boundary box appear to belong together.
- Continuity: Two intersecting straight lines are perceived as a cross, not four separate line segments meeting in the middle.
- Closure: A classic optical illusion of three black pac-man shapes facing inwards, which the brain perceives as a white triangle overlapping three black circles.
- Area: A small square overlapping a larger square is perceived as being in front.
- Symmetry: Two overlapping diamond outlines are seen as two symmetrical overlapping shapes, not three separate irregular polygons.]

--- PAGE 12 & 13 ---
Theory of visual variables (by Jaques Bertin 1967/83)
Visual variables describe the graphic dimensions across which a visualization can be varied to encode information.
7 visual variables: position, size, shape, value, orientation, color, texture.
(Motion is added as an 8th variable in computer-based visualization).
Also known as retinal variables – compared effortlessly, without additional cognitive processing.

Five key characteristics: selectivity, associativity, order, quantity, length.

--- PAGE 14 & 15 & 16 & 17 ---
Selectivity
A visual variable is selective if a single instance can be effortlessly isolated from a group.
- Selective: position, size, value, texture, hue, orientation.
- Not selective: shape.

[Image Description: 
- A slide with a dense field of scattered letters (N, M, Z, K). Finding all K's takes time because shape is not selective.
- Two slides with dense fields of overlapping, hollow geometric shapes (squares, circles, triangles, pentagons, hexagons). In the first image, all outlines are white, making it very hard to "find all the triangles". In the second image, the shapes are colored (triangles are green, squares are blue/red), making the triangles instantly pop out because color (hue) is highly selective.]

--- PAGE 18 ---
Associativity
A variable is associative if its symbols are perceived as a group despite differences in that variable.
- Associative: position, shape, orientation, hue, texture.
- Dissociative: size, value. (Small size and value interfere with perceiving other variables).

[Image Description: A mix of green circles, green triangles, orange squares, and orange circles/triangles of varying sizes. The eye can group them by color or shape easily, but very small shapes are harder to identify.]

--- PAGE 19 & 20 ---
Order and Quantity
Order: A variable is ordered if its values are immediately perceived as ranked (Less/greater).
- Ordered variables: size, position, value, texture granularity.
- Not ordered: orientation, hue, shape.

Quantity: A variable is quantitative if relative value differences are immediately readable without an index.
- Fully quantitative: Position.
- Somewhat quantitative: Size.
- Not quantitative: All other variables.

[Image Description: Scatterplot showing how position clearly shows quantity/order. Two circles of different sizes show how size indicates order and rough quantity.]

--- PAGE 21 ---
Length
Number of distinctions possible using the variable.
- Long length (many variations perceptible): shape, position, size, hue, texture, value.
- Short length (<= 4 alternatives): orientation.

Cognitive Issues & Pitfalls
Cognitive Load in Dashboards
A dashboard functions as a "cockpit" - monitoring tool for assessing system status at a glance.
Goal: Minimize cognitive load.
"Graphical excellence is that which gives to the viewer the greatest number of ideas in the shortest time with the least ink in the smallest space." (Edward Tufte)
Simplicity: Less is more. Simple designs are approachable, recognizable, immediate, and usable.

Techniques for Simpler Design
Reduction: Reducing a design to its essence, eliminate whatever isn't necessary.

Regularity
Repeat elements according to a noticeable rule or rhythm. Reduce components to basic forms, limit typography, make similar forms identical.

Use white space for grouping, not lines.

--- PAGE 34 & 35 ---
Change Blindness & Animation
Change blindness: Observers fail to notice significant changes in a visual scene due to visual interruptions (flickering, scrolling).
Solution: Animated Transitions. Animations ensure object persistence—the eye follows the motion.
[Image Description: A bar chart with 11 bars labeled A-K. One bar (H) has a line drawn through it, illustrating how a sudden update in one bar might be missed if the whole dashboard simply refreshes instantly without an animated transition to draw the eye.]

--- PAGE 36 & 37 ---
Common Deceptions (Misleading graphs)
[Image Description: Several charts showing data manipulation:
- Manipulating the y-axis (Omitting baselines): A chart showing sales between 5.5 and 7.5 million looks like a massive volatile swing because the Y-axis starts at 5.0 instead of 0. When shown starting at 0, the sales look very stable.
- Expanding or compressing the scale: Stretching the Y-axis up to 30 million makes the exact same data look like a tiny, flat, insignificant line.
- Cherry picking: Showing only sales from Jan-Jun shows a steep drop, but a zoomed-out view over a decade shows an overall rising trend.
- Flipping the graph: Reversing the Y-axis so 0 is at the top makes a rising trend look like a falling trend.
- Two y-axis: Forcing two unrelated lines (Sales and Sick calls) to overlap by manipulating their dual axes to falsely imply correlation.
- 3D pie charts: The perspective distorts the front slice making it look much larger than the back slices.]

--- PAGE 39 & 40 ---
Brushing & Linking, Direct Manipulation
- Brushing & Linking: Selecting a subset of data in one view automatically highlights the same data in other views. Requires low latency (<100ms).
- Direct Manipulation: Interact directly with graphical objects (drag, click, slide). Immediate feedback, encourages discovery.

--- PAGE 44 & 45 ---
Annotation Layers & Typography
- Annotations: Highlight, contextualize, and reduce cognitive load via direct labeling. 
- Typography: Use high-contrast text, clear fonts, and proper spacing (leading, kerning).

--- PAGE 46 & 47 ---
Modern Visualisation Contexts
Mobile First Visualization & Responsive Data
- Mobile Challenge: Limited space, fat fingers. Prioritize KPIs, vertical scrolling, micro-charts (sparklines). Thumb zone design.
- Responsive Data: Adaptive visual encoding. Shrink or change chart types based on screen size (e.g., Heatmap to Bar Chart).
[Image Description: A side-by-side comparison. Left: Mobile phone showing a single large KPI ($1.2M) and a simple sparkline trend. Right: Desktop screen showing the same KPI but alongside 2 other KPIs and a complex dual-line chart with a legend and axes. Text between them reads "Progressive Enhancement".]

--- PAGE 48 ---
Accessibility in Charts
- Color Independence: Never rely on color alone (use patterns/shapes).
- High Contrast: 3:1 for objects, 4.5:1 for text (WCAG 2.1).
- Screen Readers: Meaningful Alt-Text, accessible tables.
- Keyboard Navigation: Reachable via Tab-key.
